# The 34 UX Myths

Each entry: the popular belief, why it's wrong, and the practical takeaway. Written as an original synthesis of the myths catalogued by Zoltan Gocza and Zoltan Kollin on uxmyths.com, not a copy of their text.

---

### Myth 1, "People read on the web"
**Belief:** Visitors read your copy top to bottom like a book.
**Reality:** People scan for keywords, headings, and scannable chunks; they only read word-for-word once they've found what they were looking for. Eye-tracking studies put average on-page reading well under a quarter of the text.
**Takeaway:** Write for scanning: short paragraphs, meaningful headings, front-loaded key info. Long blocks of promotional or explanatory text get skipped.

### Myth 2, "All pages should be reachable in 3 clicks"
**Belief:** Users abandon a site if a page takes more than 3 clicks/taps to reach.
**Reality:** Click count itself doesn't predict satisfaction or success; several studies found *more* clicks (with clearer intermediate choices) outperformed fewer, confusing ones.
**Takeaway:** Optimize for confident, well-labeled steps toward the goal, not for minimizing a raw click count.

### Myth 3, "People don't scroll"
**Belief:** Everything important must fit above the fold.
**Reality:** Scrolling is now completely natural; large-scale analytics show most attention and engagement happens below the fold, and users scroll when content earns it.
**Takeaway:** Don't cram content to avoid scrolling, but the top of the page still gets the most attention, so it must earn the scroll.

### Myth 4, "Design is about making it look good"
**Belief:** Design = visual polish.
**Reality:** Design is about how something works as much as how it looks; form serves function. Good design is often invisible because it just works.
**Takeaway:** Judge a design by whether it helps users accomplish their goal, not only by its visual appeal.

### Myth 5, "Accessibility is expensive and difficult"
**Belief:** Making a product accessible requires a costly parallel effort.
**Reality:** Designed in from the start, accessibility costs about the same as inaccessible design; separating content from presentation is the core discipline, not an add-on. Retrofitting later is what gets expensive.
**Takeaway:** Bake accessibility into the initial design process; it also tends to improve SEO, performance, and overall usability.

### Myth 6, "Accessible sites are ugly"
**Belief:** Accessibility constraints force a bland, stripped-down look.
**Reality:** Accessibility governs how content is structured and exposed (semantic markup, alternative access paths), not how it's styled. Visual design and accessibility are independent axes.
**Takeaway:** You can, and should, pursue both a strong visual identity and full accessibility at once.

### Myth 7, "Graphics make an element more visible"
**Belief:** Wrapping something important in a flashy, colorful graphic will draw attention to it.
**Reality:** Users have learned to tune out anything that looks like an ad, a phenomenon called banner blindness. Graphic-heavy treatment often makes critical content *less* noticed, not more.
**Takeaway:** Use restrained contrast and text-based emphasis for important content; avoid ad-like visual patterns for anything users actually need to see.

### Myth 8, "Stock photos improve the experience"
**Belief:** Decorative photography makes a page more appealing and trustworthy.
**Reality:** Eye-tracking shows purely decorative images are largely ignored, and generic stock photography can even read as filler or reduce credibility.
**Takeaway:** Only use imagery that carries real information (product, people, data); cut anything purely ornamental.

### Myth 9, "Design has to be original"
**Belief:** Reusing familiar UI patterns is uncreative or lazy.
**Reality:** Established interface conventions work precisely because users already know them, no learning curve required. Novelty for its own sake adds friction.
**Takeaway:** Default to well-tested patterns; only break convention when you're confident the new approach is a genuine improvement, not just different.

### Myth 10, "If the design is good, small details don't matter"
**Belief:** Micro-details (wording, error messages, minor layout choices) are negligible next to the "big" design decisions.
**Reality:** Small changes, a clearer optional-field label, a better-worded error message, a de-emphasized signup step, have repeatedly driven measurable, sometimes very large, gains in conversion and satisfaction.
**Takeaway:** Treat copy, microcopy, and small interaction details as first-class design work, not afterthoughts.

### Myth 11, "You need to redesign periodically"
**Belief:** A periodic full visual overhaul keeps a product competitive.
**Reality:** Users are creatures of habit and often resent sweeping redesigns, even objectively better ones, because they disrupt learned navigation. Sites that stay successful long-term tend to evolve incrementally instead.
**Takeaway:** Prefer continuous, incremental refinement (realigning) over big-bang redesigns; reserve major overhauls for when the underlying model has genuinely stopped working.

### Myth 12, "More choices/features means more satisfaction"
**Belief:** Users are happier with a richer feature set and more options.
**Reality:** Before using a product, people say they want more choice; after using it, usability and simplicity drive actual satisfaction. Too many options create decision paralysis (see Hick's Law).
**Takeaway:** Resist feature creep. Default to fewer, well-chosen options and smart defaults rather than exposing every possible choice.

### Myth 13, "Icons enhance usability"
**Belief:** Icon-only controls are cleaner and just as clear as labeled ones.
**Reality:** Most icons have no universally agreed meaning and are hard to memorize; classic case studies (e.g. toolbar redesigns) show usage jumps dramatically once text labels are added, while re-arranging or re-drawing icons alone does little.
**Takeaway:** Pair icons with text labels except for a small set of near-universal symbols (play/pause, print, close, share); when in doubt, a label alone beats an icon alone.

### Myth 14, "You are like your users"
**Belief:** Because you understand the product deeply, your intuitions about usability transfer to your users.
**Reality:** Building or working on a product makes you atypical by definition; the "false-consensus" bias makes it easy to assume others think and want what you do.
**Takeaway:** Validate assumptions with real user research and testing rather than personal intuition, especially for anything outside your own expertise or context.

### Myth 15, "Users make optimal choices"
**Belief:** Given a page, users evaluate all options and pick the objectively best one.
**Reality:** Users "satisfice": they take the first option that seems good enough, rather than analyzing every choice. Scanning is fast and approximate.
**Takeaway:** Make the best choice the most visible and easiest to reach; don't rely on the user finding the ideal option buried in a list.

### Myth 16, "Search will save a bad information architecture"
**Belief:** If navigation is confusing, just add a search box.
**Reality:** Most users try navigation first and fall back to search only when they fail. A search box can't compensate for a structure that makes content unfindable by browsing.
**Takeaway:** Fix the IA first, then support it with search. Both are needed, but navigation is the foundation.

### Myth 17, "The homepage is the most important page"
**Belief:** The homepage is where users start and what they judge you by.
**Reality:** Most traffic enters through deep links (search results, shared links, bookmarks); the homepage is often not the first page seen. Interior and landing pages get more total attention.
**Takeaway:** Invest in the quality of every entry page, not just the homepage.

### Myth 18, "Flash is necessary for a rich experience"
**Belief:** You need plugin-based technology for animations and rich interactivity.
**Reality:** Modern HTML5, CSS, and JavaScript deliver everything Flash once offered, with better performance, accessibility, and device support.
**Takeaway:** Use modern web standards for all interactive and animated elements.

### Myth 19, "You don't need the content to design a website"
**Belief:** You can design the layout first and drop in copy later.
**Reality:** Design without real content produces layouts that break the moment real headlines, real data, and real edge cases arrive. Content shapes the design, not the other way around.
**Takeaway:** Design with real (or realistic) content from the start; avoid lorem ipsum for anything beyond initial wireframing.

### Myth 20, "If it works for Amazon, it will work for you"
**Belief:** Copying the UI patterns of a massive, successful platform guarantees success.
**Reality:** What works for a dominant marketplace with massive traffic, infinite SKUs, and years of A/B testing doesn't transfer to a different product, audience, or context.
**Takeaway:** Learn from large platforms' research methods, not their specific UI patterns. Test what works for your product and your users.

### Myth 21, "People can tell you what they want"
**Belief:** Asking users what they want (surveys, interviews) gives you reliable feature priorities.
**Reality:** What users say they want and what actually helps them often diverge. Stated preferences skew toward the familiar; real needs emerge from observing behavior on real tasks.
**Takeaway:** Combine stated feedback with behavioral data and usability observation; don't rely on interviews alone.

### Myth 22, "Usability testing is expensive"
**Belief:** You need a lab, specialized equipment, and large sample sizes to run a usability test.
**Reality:** Even quick, informal tests with 5 users catch the majority of critical usability issues. Remote, unmoderated testing tools have made it even cheaper.
**Takeaway:** Test early and often, even informally. Five real users beating on a prototype is worth more than months of internal debate.

### Myth 23, "Choices should always be limited to 7 plus/minus 2"
**Belief:** Miller's "7 plus/minus 2" means you should never have more than 7 items in a menu or list.
**Reality:** Miller's Law is about working-memory *chunking*, not a hard cap on visible options. Well-organized, scannable lists of 10+ items can work perfectly well.
**Takeaway:** Use chunking and clear structure rather than artificially cutting options to 7. The quality of organization matters more than the raw count.

### Myth 24, "People always use your product the way you intended"
**Belief:** Users follow the designed "happy path" and use features as documented.
**Reality:** Users find workarounds, use features in unexpected ways, skip steps, and create their own workflows. Rigid flows break when reality intrudes.
**Takeaway:** Design for flexibility and observe real usage; don't assume the intended flow is the only flow.

### Myth 25, "Aesthetics are not important"
**Belief:** Only function matters; visual design is superficial.
**Reality:** Visual appeal directly affects perceived usability, trust, and willingness to engage. The Aesthetic-Usability Effect is well-documented.
**Takeaway:** Invest in visual quality alongside functional quality; they reinforce each other.

### Myth 26, "Usability testing = focus groups"
**Belief:** A group discussion about the product is the same as usability testing.
**Reality:** Focus groups collect opinions and attitudes; usability testing observes individual behavior on real tasks. They answer fundamentally different questions.
**Takeaway:** Use usability testing for interface evaluation and focus groups for attitude/brand research; don't conflate them.

### Myth 27, "UX design is a step in a project"
**Belief:** UX is a phase you complete (like wireframing) and then move on.
**Reality:** UX is a continuous practice that spans the entire lifecycle: research, design, development, launch, and iteration.
**Takeaway:** Build UX into every phase of the project, not just the beginning.

### Myth 28, "White space is wasted space"
**Belief:** Every pixel should contain content or controls; empty space is inefficiency.
**Reality:** White space improves readability, scannability, and focus. Cramming content reduces comprehension.
**Takeaway:** Treat whitespace as a deliberate design element, not waste. It directs attention and reduces cognitive load.

### Myth 29, "If it's a good design, users won't need instructions"
**Belief:** Truly intuitive interfaces require zero guidance.
**Reality:** Even well-designed interfaces benefit from contextual help, tooltips, and progressive disclosure for complex or novel tasks.
**Takeaway:** Provide lightweight, contextual guidance (not heavy documentation) even in well-designed UIs, especially for domain-specific workflows.

### Myth 30, "If you are an expert, you don't need to test"
**Belief:** Deep domain expertise substitutes for observing real users.
**Reality:** Expertise creates blind spots: you can't unsee what you know. Testing catches issues that expertise alone misses.
**Takeaway:** Test with real users regardless of how experienced the team is.

### Myth 31, "UX design is about usability"
**Belief:** If users can complete tasks, the UX is good.
**Reality:** UX encompasses emotion, meaning, and perceived value, not just task completion. A usable product can still feel frustrating, confusing, or untrustworthy.
**Takeaway:** Evaluate UX on desirability and emotional impact alongside pure usability.

### Myth 32, "Success depends on how good a product is"
**Belief:** Build a great product and users will come.
**Reality:** Distribution, timing, marketing, and context often outweigh product quality alone. Many great products fail for non-product reasons.
**Takeaway:** Product quality is necessary but not sufficient; consider the full go-to-market picture.

### Myth 33, "Online shopping experience should replicate real-world shopping"
**Belief:** Digital interfaces should mirror physical store experiences.
**Reality:** Digital and physical affordances are fundamentally different. What works in a store (browsing shelves) doesn't map to what works on screen (filtering, search, recommendations).
**Takeaway:** Design for the medium's strengths rather than imitating a physical analogy.

### Myth 34, "Simple = minimal"
**Belief:** The simplest design is the one with the fewest elements.
**Reality:** "Simple" means "easy to use," which sometimes requires *more* visible UI (labels, guidance, explicit options), not less. Hiding too much in the name of minimalism creates confusion.
**Takeaway:** Optimize for ease of use, not visual minimalism. If a label, tooltip, or extra control makes the task clearer, add it.
